Your cat may come home with several medicines and a plan that feels hard to keep straight. Start with one question for each item: **“What is this meant to do for my cat?”** Easing pain, treating an infection and preventing another blockage are different goals. A study measuring one does not settle the others.

**Repeated straining with little or no urine, or uncertainty about urine during repeated attempts, needs immediate veterinary assessment—even while your cat is receiving treatment. Contact the clinic or an emergency service now.** [Cornell’s guidance on urinary obstruction](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-lower-urinary-tract-disease)

## What question is each medicine trying to answer?

Use the purpose written in your cat’s own plan. The categories below explain the evidence, rather than recommending a medicine.

| Treatment category | Intended purpose to clarify | What a study may measure instead |
|---|---|---|
| Pain relief | Comfort during a painful illness or recovery | Whether another blockage occurred, which is a different outcome |
| Prazosin | Relaxing smooth muscle around part of the urinary outlet | Whether treated cats had fewer repeat obstructions |
| Antibiotics | Treating a bacterial infection when clinically indicated | Bacterial growth, symptom response or recurrence; each needs context |
| Supplements | A product-specific proposed effect | Changes in urine measurements or signs, sometimes in a different population |

The [2025 iCatCare guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/) prioritizes pain care and assesses recurrence evidence separately. [VCA’s prazosin handout](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/prazosin) explains its muscle-relaxing purpose; that mechanism is not proof of prevention.

## Why might I read conflicting things about prazosin?

Prazosin has been used after blockage, but the prevention evidence has not supported routine use. In a blinded randomized trial of cats with a **first obstruction**, it did not demonstrate fewer repeat blockages than placebo. That selected study population and its exclusions matter when interpreting the result. [Hanson and colleagues, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33749375/)

The 2025 guideline says prazosin is **not generally recommended on current evidence**, while noting unanswered questions about whether some groups might benefit. [iCatCare’s evidence assessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/)

For an existing prescription, the useful conversation is: “What specific benefit are you aiming for, how will it be assessed, and what problems should prompt a call?” A research summary cannot make that individual prescription decision. VCA identifies marked weakness, fainting and excessive tiredness as reasons for immediate veterinary contact. [VCA’s safety information](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/prazosin)

## Does a negative meloxicam study mean pain relief is unnecessary?

No. Meloxicam is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, or NSAID. A small randomized trial in **37 cats with obstructive idiopathic cystitis** did not detect a reduction in repeat blockage with meloxicam. Both groups also received other supportive care. That trial does not settle every pain-management question or show that every pain medicine has the same benefits and risks. [Dorsch and colleagues, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132225/)

Country and product matter. The **US FDA** warns that repeated meloxicam use in cats has been associated with sudden kidney failure and death; oral products are not FDA-approved for cats. [FDA safety information](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/product-safety-information/information-about-boxed-warning-meloxicam-labels-regarding-safety-risks-cats)

In **Great Britain**, particular feline oral products have authorized pain indications and their own cautions. That does not establish a repeat-blockage-prevention indication or make products and instructions interchangeable across countries. [VMD product information, revised September 2025](https://www.vmd.defra.gov.uk/productinformationdatabase/files/SPC_Documents/SPC_2802492.PDF)

Bring the exact product and your written instructions to any discussion of pain care, especially when different clinics have been involved.

## Are antibiotics part of every urinary plan?

Antibiotics address bacterial infection. **Cystitis** means bladder inflammation; **FLUTD**, short for feline lower urinary tract disease, is an umbrella term for several conditions. Neither label, by itself, establishes a bacterial infection. Culture results need interpretation with the signs and sample context. Bacteria can also be found without clinical infection, when antibiotics are generally discouraged. [ISCAID infection guideline](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2019.02.008)

Ask: “Was infection confirmed or suspected? Is a result pending, and who will explain whether it changes the plan?” If symptoms return after treatment, that is information for reassessment, rather than proof that the same infection has returned. The [diagnosis and testing guide](/learn/cats/urinary-health/fic-flutd-recurrent-urinary-signs-record/) explains those distinctions.

## What about cranberry and other urinary supplements?

The relevant question is what the **particular formulation** has shown in cats like yours. A small study allocated **21 cats with feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC)** to a cranberry-containing supplement group or control group. Both groups improved on some signs by day 30. The preliminary report does not establish that cranberry alone, or a different commercial product, prevents obstruction. [Colombino and colleagues, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34039227/)

“Urinary support” on a label is not a study outcome. Before adding a product, give the clinic its name and ingredients so it can be considered alongside the prescribed food and medicines. A supplement question should not delay assessment of new urinary signs.

## Make the plan easier to discuss

Keep one record of the exact products prescribed, the clinic’s instructions and what actually happened when giving them. The [vet-visit record](/learn/cats/urinary-health/vet-visit-record/) can help. Note a missed or vomited medicine, difficulty administering it, poor appetite or unusual behavior; contact the treating team for the next step rather than guessing a replacement plan. A record helps explain the problem without making you responsible for diagnosing its cause.

Before the next appointment ends, get a clear answer to: **“What is each item for, what should I watch for, and who do I contact if the plan is difficult to follow?”**
