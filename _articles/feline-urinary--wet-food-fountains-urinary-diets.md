Your cat needs urinary food—but will not eat it. Or perhaps you are wondering whether an ordinary wet food could do the same job for less. The most useful first question is **what is this food meant to do for your cat's diagnosis?**

Wet food supplies moisture. That does not, on its own, make it a substitute for a food selected to dissolve a particular stone or meet another treatment goal. You need a plan that fits the diagnosis **and** that your cat can eat. If the current plan is failing, tell the clinic so you can work out an alternative together.

**If your cat repeatedly strains and passes little or no urine—or you cannot tell whether urine is coming out—contact a vet or emergency clinic immediately. Food and extra water cannot relieve a blockage.** [International Cat Care's urinary-disease guidance](https://icatcare.org/resources/cat-carer-guide-urinary-tract-diseases.pdf)

## Start with the diagnosis, then the food

“Urinary problem” describes a concern, but it does not tell you which food is appropriate. Use this table to clarify the plan with your treating team.

| What you have been told | What that means for the food conversation |
|---|---|
| **Crystals were seen in urine** | Crystals do not, by themselves, establish that stones are present or what a stone is made of. Ask what the finding changes about treatment. |
| **Struvite stones are suspected** | A suitable treatment diet can dissolve struvite stones. Ask how imaging will check whether the stones are shrinking. |
| **Calcium oxalate stones were found** | These do not dissolve with diet. Ask about removal or monitoring, and the separate goal of reducing future stone formation. |
| **Feline idiopathic cystitis, or FIC** | This is a painful condition diagnosed after other causes have been considered. Food belongs within a broader plan, including pain care and changes that help your cat feel secure at home. |

These distinctions come from the [2025 iCatCare clinical guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/). If your cat has had a blockage, also ask what caused it and which findings the food recommendation is addressing. Our [guide to diagnosis and tests](/learn/cats/urinary-health/fic-flutd-recurrent-urinary-signs-record/) explains the terms you may hear.

## Can ordinary wet food replace prescribed urinary food?

There is no reliable yes-or-no answer without knowing the treatment goal. Moisture content is one feature of a food; the whole formulation matters. The word “urinary” on a package does not establish that two foods were designed or tested for the same purpose.

For example, a clinical trial compared **two dry treatment foods** in 37 cats with presumed struvite stones. Stones completely dissolved in 32 cats; the five nonresponders had other stone types. This supports those foods' dissolution role in the studied cats. It was not a test of ordinary wet food, and it did not establish lifelong protection from blockage. [Lulich and colleagues, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24094262/)

Before substituting a food, get a clear answer to: **“Are we treating an existing stone, managing FIC, reducing future stone formation, or meeting more than one goal?”** Then ask which alternatives meet that same goal.

## What does the wet-food evidence actually show?

Two claims that sound similar can mean different things: “this food changed the urine” and “fewer cats became ill again.” Both matter, but one does not automatically establish the other.

| What researchers studied | What the result can tell us |
|---|---|
| **Urine measurements in healthy cats** | In a 2024 experiment with 36 healthy cats, higher-moisture foods increased urine volume and reduced measured mineral supersaturation—a laboratory estimate of how readily crystals might form. The experiment did not measure fewer FIC flare-ups or blockages. [Original feeding study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0315949) |
| **Return of signs in cats with FIC** | In a 1999 study, 46 of 54 cats were followed for a year. No recurrence was reported in 16/18 cats eating a particular canned treatment food and 17/28 eating its dry version. Incomplete follow-up and uncertainty about allocation limit the comparison. It was not a trial of every wet food or of blockage prevention. [Original study record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10023397/) |

A [2025 systematic review of recurrent FIC management](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/items/82482980-61c2-4ae2-b2df-f0041e01dbd0) found the strongest available support for therapeutic urinary foods, particularly with increased moisture, together with changes to reduce stress and conflict in the home. It also emphasized the shortage of high-quality studies. That is a reason to discuss these approaches as part of a plan; it is not a promise that a food will prevent another episode.

When reading a food claim, look for four details: **which cats, which formulation, which outcome and how long they were followed.** A lower laboratory “crystal risk” number is not a count of emergencies prevented.

## What if my cat refuses the urinary food?

Food refusal is a practical problem the treatment plan needs to solve. **Do not withhold food to force acceptance.** International Cat Care advises discussing alternatives, including different textures or formulations, with the veterinary team. [Caregiver feeding guidance](https://icatcare.org/resources/cat-carer-guide-urinary-tract-diseases.pdf)

Contact the clinic if your cat is eating poorly. Describe whether they reject only the new food or are eating less overall, what they have actually eaten, and any vomiting, discomfort or urinary signs. This helps the team decide whether the problem is food acceptance, illness or both.

Ask for a written plan covering:

- What should I offer while we arrange a suitable alternative?
- Which other foods or textures fit this treatment goal?
- Must this food be fed exclusively? What about treats or food used for medicines?
- How should we make any agreed change, and how will we check that my cat is eating enough?
- Whom should I contact if poor appetite continues, including out of hours?

Do not wait for a food trial to solve repeated straining with little or uncertain urine output. That needs immediate veterinary contact.

## Will my cat need urinary food for life?

Not every urinary-food recommendation is lifelong. International Cat Care recommends reviewing the choice with the veterinary team. [Caregiver guide](https://icatcare.org/resources/cat-carer-guide-urinary-tract-diseases.pdf)

Ask the clinic to distinguish the current treatment phase from the longer-term plan: **“What result are we waiting for, when will we review the food, and what would justify changing it?”** Do not assume a better-looking litter tray means a stone has dissolved or the plan is finished.

## What about kidney disease, other cats or cost?

**Another illness:** “Urinary” and “kidney” are not interchangeable treatment goals. If your cat has kidney disease, heart disease or another condition, bring the exact food names and medicine list to the clinic. Ask which goals take priority and how follow-up tests will check the chosen plan.

**A shared food bowl:** Ask whether each cat can eat the proposed food, given their age, life stage and health. If exclusive feeding matters, tell the team how food is shared now and ask for an arrangement you can realistically maintain.

**An unaffordable plan:** Say so early. Ask for suitable alternatives, the expected daily quantity, follow-up costs and a review date. Cost and food acceptance are part of making treatment workable; they are useful information for the team.

## Why do some urinary foods contain more salt?

Some formulations use sodium to encourage drinking, but the measured effects vary. One short study in healthy cats found more urine and lower mineral supersaturation; another found more urine and calcium excretion, with **no change in calcium oxalate supersaturation**. [Quéau and colleagues, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32157751/), [Paßlack and colleagues, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24881513/)

A two-year study of 20 healthy older cats did not detect increased blood-pressure or heart-function harm at the tested salt level. That does not establish safety for a cat with heart or kidney disease, or for adding salt at home. Ask about the **complete food and your cat's health**, rather than trying to adjust individual nutrients yourself. [Chetboul and colleagues, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24941013/)

## Will a fountain make my cat drink more?

A fountain may suit your cat's preference. It is not a proven way to prevent another urinary episode.

In one study, 14 healthy laboratory cats completed comparisons of still, circulating and free-falling water. Average drinking, urine volume and several urine measures did not significantly differ. One concentration measure, **urine osmolality**, was higher with circulating water; the clinical meaning was unclear. Three cats showed individual preferences. [Robbins and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30303439/)

In another study, 12 healthy pet cats completed a bowl-versus-fountain comparison. Drinking was slightly greater with the fountain, without a significant difference in urine osmolality. Neither study tested whether fountains prevented disease in cats with urinary problems. [Grant, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20005758/)

If you try a fountain, treat it as an additional choice and keep a familiar, accepted water source available. The useful question is whether **your cat comfortably uses it**, alongside the rest of the agreed care plan.

“This is what my cat will eat, this is how our household feeds them, and this is what we can afford. **Which workable plan meets the treatment goal, and how will we know it is helping?**”
