**Repeated straining with little or no urine—or uncertainty about whether urine is coming out during repeated attempts—needs immediate veterinary assessment. Contact your veterinarian or an emergency clinic now.** Possible blockage is an emergency; care should not wait for a sample or a completed diary. [Cornell’s urinary-health guidance](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-lower-urinary-tract-disease)

Once your cat has been assessed, the words can still be confusing. A discharge note may mention cystitis, crystals and FLUTD, while you have been calling the problem a UTI. These labels describe different things. The useful starting question is: **“What have we found, what do we currently think is causing the signs, and what remains uncertain?”**

## Which words describe a cause?

Blood in urine, frequent litter-box or litter-tray visits and accidents around the house are signs to report. Several conditions can cause them, so their appearance alone cannot identify the disease. [Cornell’s explanation of urinary signs](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-lower-urinary-tract-disease)

| Word in the record | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **FLUTD** | Feline lower urinary tract disease: an umbrella for disorders of the bladder and urethra, not one underlying cause. |
| **Cystitis** | Bladder inflammation. The word does not automatically mean bacterial infection. |
| **FIC** | Feline idiopathic cystitis, a painful condition diagnosed after other explanations have been considered and excluded. |
| **Bacterial UTI** | Bacterial infection in the urinary tract. Bacterial cystitis involves the bladder. |
| **Crystalluria** | Crystals seen in a urine sample. |
| **Uroliths** | Urinary stones: solid mineral formations. |
| **Urethral obstruction** | A blockage that prevents normal urine flow; an emergency. |

These distinctions follow the [2025 iCatCare diagnostic guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/). A finding such as crystals and a working diagnosis such as FIC may appear in the same record. Ask how the veterinarian thinks they relate in this episode.

## What is each test trying to answer?

Investigations are selected for the individual cat. This map helps you understand the plan; it is not a list every cat must have. Cornell describes examination and urinalysis, with other tests when needed. [Cornell’s diagnostic overview](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-lower-urinary-tract-disease)

| Investigation | Purpose and useful question | Limit to clarify |
|---|---|---|
| Examination and history | “What needs attention now, and which possible causes are you investigating?” | What still needs testing? |
| Urinalysis | Looks at urine concentration, chemistry and microscopic findings. “What did it show?” | It is not the same as a bacterial culture. |
| Culture and susceptibility testing | Investigates bacterial growth and helps guide antibiotic selection when relevant. “How does this result fit the signs?” | Collection method and clinical context affect interpretation. |
| X-rays or ultrasound | Investigates stones and structural changes. “What did this examination assess?” | Different examinations provide different information. |
| Blood tests | Checks kidney function or other health issues that may affect care. | A blood result does not name every possible bladder problem. |

The culture distinction is explained in the [ISCAID infection guideline](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2019.02.008). Ask for the result in ordinary language as well as a copy of the report. “Normal” is more useful when you know which question the test answered.

## Does “suspected FIC” mean the tests failed?

FIC has no single clinically available confirmatory test. Diagnosis draws on the history, findings and exclusion of other causes. A working diagnosis states the best current explanation, while retaining uncertainty. It does not mean that the pain is imaginary or that you have failed to identify a stressful event. [2025 iCatCare guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/)

Try: “How confident are we in FIC, and what result or change would make you reconsider?” If signs return, bring the previous record; the old label is useful background, rather than something you need to prove still applies.

## Do crystals prove there is a stone?

No. A crystal finding does not establish an obstructing stone. Stones can occur without matching crystals in the urine. [2025 iCatCare guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/)

Sample handling matters too. In a laboratory study of urine from **41 cats without a history of urinary disease**, storing divided samples changed crystal detection. That finding concerns how a specimen is interpreted; it does not show that a food prevents illness. [Sturgess and colleagues, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11876621/)

Useful questions are: “Were stones found or suspected? Are these crystals thought relevant? What follow-up would check the treatment goal?”

## If bacteria grew, are antibiotics always needed?

Bacteria can be present without signs attributable to infection. This is called **subclinical bacteriuria**; antibiotic treatment is generally discouraged in that situation, with exceptions requiring clinical judgment. A positive culture therefore needs interpretation, not an automatic owner treatment decision. [ISCAID guideline](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2019.02.008)

If a “UTI” keeps returning, ask whether infection was confirmed, how the sample was collected, and whether further investigation is needed. Medicine changes belong in that discussion with the prescriber.

Before leaving, write down the working diagnosis, confirmed findings, any pending results and the follow-up plan. The [vet-visit record](/learn/cats/urinary-health/vet-visit-record/) has space to keep those details together. Finish with: “What change means I should call immediately?” Your job is to share observations clearly; the treating team’s job is to interpret them.
