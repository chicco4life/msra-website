**If your cat repeatedly tries to pee but passes little or no urine, contact your vet or an emergency clinic now for immediate assessment. If you cannot tell whether urine is coming out during those attempts, explain that when you call.** A urinary blockage can become life-threatening, and waiting to see whether it improves can delay needed treatment. [PDSA: blocked bladder in cats](https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/pet-health-hub/conditions/blocked-bladder-in-cats)

You do not have to work out whether this is a blockage, a urine infection or constipation before calling. Describe the attempts and what you have seen coming out. The veterinary team can decide what needs to happen next.

## What counts as a worrying change?

You might notice repeated litter-box or litter-tray visits, a long time spent trying to urinate, tiny wet spots, crying or licking around the genitals. These signs can occur with several urinary conditions. They do not identify the cause, but repeated attempts with little or no urine need urgent attention. [MSPCA-Angell: urethral obstruction](https://www.mspca.org/clinical/urethral-obstruction-in-cats/)

Tell the clinic about blood, vomiting, hiding or a change in appetite or activity too. Do not wait for your cat to become visibly very ill before seeking help.

## Is my cat trying to pee or poop?

Straining to urinate can look like straining to pass a stool. If you are unsure, say so. A possible urinary problem needs assessment before you choose a constipation treatment. [MSPCA-Angell](https://www.mspca.org/clinical/urethral-obstruction-in-cats/)

Do not press or squeeze your cat’s abdomen to test the bladder or try to force urine out. This can cause pain and injury; examination belongs with the veterinary team. [2025 iCatCare guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/)

## What if my cat passed urine earlier or still seems well?

Tell the clinic about the **current** pattern. Earlier urine, a few drops or a normal appetite cannot establish that your cat is safe now. Male cats are particularly susceptible to blockage, but a cat with concerning signs needs assessment regardless of sex. [Cornell Feline Health Center](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-lower-urinary-tract-disease)

If several cats share a tray, or your cat toilets outside, it may be hard to know what has been passed. “I cannot tell whose urine this is” is useful information. You do not need a completed diary or another observed attempt before making the call.

## What to say when you call

Start with the concern, then the details:

> “My cat keeps trying to urinate. I have seen [drops/no urine/uncertain output]. This started [when]. The last urine I am sure came from this cat was [when/unknown]. My cat [has/has not] recently been treated for a urinary blockage.”

Add any vomiting, weakness, pain, blood and current medicines. Ask where to bring your cat. If transport or payment is an obstacle, explain that during the call so the clinic can discuss the immediate options. Do not postpone contact while searching for funding or collecting a urine sample.

## Has your cat recently had a catheter?

Repeated straining with little or no urine after treatment needs the same immediate contact. Catheter removal does not rule out another blockage. Ongoing urinary signs can have other explanations too, which the clinic needs to distinguish. A generic recovery timeline cannot settle this at home. [2025 iCatCare guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/)

The [recovery guide](/learn/cats/urinary-health/after-urinary-blockage/) explains what to report and why repeat symptoms and another confirmed blockage are different.
