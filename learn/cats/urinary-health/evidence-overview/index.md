After a urinary blockage, it is natural to want one clear rule that will keep your cat safe: a different food, more water, perhaps a medicine. Research can help you make sense of those choices, but they do different jobs. The starting point is the cause of your cat's problem and the purpose of each part of the treatment plan.

This overview separates three questions that often get mixed together: **Can my cat pass urine now? What caused the problem? What might reduce future episodes?** A finding about one question may not answer the others.

**If your cat is repeatedly trying to pee and passing little or no urine, contact a veterinary clinic immediately. If you cannot tell whether urine is coming out, explain that when you call. Do not wait for this guide or a change of food to solve it.** [PDSA: blocked bladder in cats](https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/pet-health-hub/conditions/blocked-bladder-in-cats)

<a id="diagnosis-and-stones"></a>

## First, find out what the diagnosis means

Similar signs can have different causes. “FLUTD,” short for feline lower urinary tract disease, describes a group of problems rather than one final diagnosis. FIC—feline idiopathic cystitis—is a particular condition diagnosed after considering other causes. It is not another name for a bacterial infection. Crystals in a sample and stones in the bladder are also different findings. [2025 iCatCare consensus guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816079/)

The useful question is: **“What have we found, what is still suspected, and which part of the plan addresses each problem?”** Your answer may change as test results return. The [diagnosis guide](/learn/cats/urinary-health/fic-flutd-recurrent-urinary-signs-record/) shows what individual tests can and cannot tell you.

<a id="food-and-water"></a>

## Food research measures more than one kind of benefit

More water in food can change water intake and urine measurements. For example, a 2024 experiment in 36 healthy cats found that higher-moisture diets increased water intake and urine volume and lowered a laboratory measure of crystal-forming tendency. It did not test whether cats had fewer blockages. [Gonçalves Tozato and colleagues, 2024](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0315949)

Stone dissolution is a different question. In a 2011 study, stones dissolved in 17 of 21 cats given the test diets; the four nonresponders had other stone types. That supports a diagnosis-specific food plan, rather than treating every food with “urinary” on the label as interchangeable. [Houston and colleagues, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22005442/)

<a id="recurring-fic"></a>

For recurrent FIC, a published 2025 systematic review identified therapeutic urinary foods, especially with more dietary moisture, and tailored changes to the home environment as the approaches with the strongest available support. It also highlighted the lack of high-quality studies. Both parts matter: these are reasonable approaches to discuss, without a promise that they will prevent every episode. [Macleod and colleagues, 2025](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/items/82482980-61c2-4ae2-b2df-f0041e01dbd0)

Our [food guide](/learn/cats/urinary-health/wet-food-fountains-urinary-diets/) connects those findings to the harder household questions: refusal, shared food, costs and other illnesses.

## A fountain is an option to assess, rather than a guarantee

Small studies of healthy cats have compared fountains and bowls. Their findings about drinking and urine concentration vary; neither established prevention of urinary blockage. An individual cat's preference can differ from the average study result. [Robbins and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30303439/), [Grant, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20005758/)

The practical issue is whether your cat accepts the available water sources and how they fit the wider plan. Buying a fountain does not answer why your cat had urinary signs.

<a id="after-a-blockage"></a>

## A medicine's purpose matters as much as its name

A medicine may be prescribed to relieve pain, address an infection or support another part of care. Preventing a repeat blockage is a separate outcome.

Prazosin illustrates this distinction. A 2021 placebo-controlled trial did not demonstrate fewer repeat blockages in the cats receiving prazosin. That result does not decide every possible use of the medicine or tell an owner to stop it. The useful next step is to ask the prescriber what benefit is expected for this cat and when the prescription should be reviewed. [Hanson and colleagues, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33749375/)

## Make the plan work for your household

Bring up the details that are difficult: a cat refusing the food, trouble giving tablets, several cats sharing a tray, the cost of follow-up or getting to an emergency clinic. Those details help the team make the plan usable. You do not need to arrive with a diagnosis of your own.

The [free vet-visit record](/learn/cats/urinary-health/vet-visit-record/) gives you a place for observations, the clinic's instructions and your questions. Write “unknown” when you cannot tell what happened. Completing a record must never delay urgent care.

## What MSRA's review can tell you

MSRA explains published work by other researchers. This collection is an evidence review with incomplete source access and appraisal; it is not an MSRA treatment trial or a newly completed, fully appraised systematic review. The [technical review](/research/2026-08-feline-urinary-health/) explains those limits, and the [source index](/learn/cats/urinary-health/studies/) lets you find the original publications.

When you see a new claim, ask: **Were these cats like mine? Did the study measure actual illness or a urine test? What was the treatment compared with?** Those three questions make the research more useful at your next consultation.
